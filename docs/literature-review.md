# Literature review and academic positioning

## The existing scholarship answers a different question

The study of Kenyan running is not a blank field. A mature body of work, largely physiological and sociocultural, has spent nearly two decades explaining **why** Kenyans — and specifically Kalenjin and Nandi-subtribe runners — dominate distance events:

- **Onywera, Scott, Boit & Pitsiladis (2006), *Journal of Sports Sciences*** — "Demographic characteristics of elite Kenyan endurance runners." Established the geographic and socioeconomic concentration of elite runners, and that a large share train and compete primarily for economic advancement.
- **Wilber & Pitsiladis (2012), *International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance*** — "Kenyan and Ethiopian distance runners: what makes them so good?" A holistic review of altitude, biomechanics, training, and motivation.
- **Tucker, Onywera & Santos-Concejero (2015), *IJSPP*** — "Analysis of the Kenyan Distance-Running Phenomenon." Tracked the increasing share of championship medals won by Kalenjin/Nandi runners from Kenya's first medal in 1964 onward.

This literature is rigorous and should be cited precisely. It is also, deliberately, **not what this project is about.** The physiology-and-genetics question is well covered and contested; re-deriving it would add nothing.

## The gap this project occupies

What the existing literature does not systematically address is the **governance, integrity, and welfare dimension** of Kenyan Olympic success:

- Where sponsorship and public money actually goes (the Nike/Athletics Kenya diversion; the Rio 2016 and Wario funds cases).
- How the anti-doping system collapsed toward WADA non-compliance in 2016 and what the remediation has and hasn't achieved.
- The structural economics of doping among a uniquely deep sub-elite class, as framed by the Athletics Integrity Unit.
- Athlete welfare and its gendered dimension, brought into focus by the killing of Agnes Tirop and the documented exploitation of female athletes as primary breadwinners.
- The state's evolving policy response — reward structures, welfare trusts, and budget allocations — and whether it is structural reform or reactive gesture.

There is emerging work on some of these threads individually (e.g. anti-doping education research, athlete labour-migration studies, and a small legal literature on African sport governance such as the 2024 *International Sports Law Journal* work on political neutrality in African federations). What does not exist is a **single, open, structured, source-linked dataset** that binds the medal record to these governance and welfare threads and makes the relationships analysable.

## Publication strategy

**Track A — the dataset as a citable artifact.** Release the medal, controversy, and policy datasets on Zenodo with a DOI. This makes the data independently citable before any narrative paper is complete and is the fastest route to academic standing.

**Track B — the analytical manuscript.** Primary target: **International Journal of Sport Policy and Politics** (Taylor & Francis; CiteScore ~3.3; SJR Q1 Social Sciences). Its stated scope explicitly solicits work on the role and influence of national government in sport policy and on government as regulator and resource provider — a direct fit. The journal also runs a **Country Profile** format designed to document a nation's sport-policy landscape, a realistic first submission while the full research article matures.

Secondary target for the welfare/gender material specifically: **Journal of Sport and Social Issues**, a better home for the athlete-exploitation analysis than a policy-mechanics journal.

## What makes the manuscript competitive rather than descriptive

A chronology of scandals is not publishable research. A testable claim, evaluated against an open dataset, is. The `funding_vs_output.csv` layer exists to support exactly this: hypotheses of the form *"governance scandals cluster in pre-Games years and are followed by short-lived funding increases without structural reform,"* tested against committed data. Reviewers at a policy journal want a mechanism and an evidence base — which is what the dataset design is built to provide.
